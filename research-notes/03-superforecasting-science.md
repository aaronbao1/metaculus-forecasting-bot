## 1. Tetlock & the Good Judgment Project (GJP)

### 1.1 The Ten Commandments for Aspiring Superforecasters
From the appendix of Tetlock & Gardner, *Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction* (2015). Full list (verbatim headings, condensed glosses):

1. **Triage.** Focus on questions in the "Goldilocks zone" of difficulty — not clocklike questions where rules of thumb get you close, not impenetrable "cloud-like" ones where nothing beats the dart-throwing chimp. Effort pays off most in between.
2. **Break seemingly intractable problems into tractable sub-problems.** (Fermi-ize; expose hidden assumptions and "guesstimate" the parts.)
3. **Strike the right balance between inside and outside views.** Nothing is 100% unique — pose the outside-view question first: "How often do things of this sort happen in situations of this sort?" Then adjust with case specifics.
4. **Strike the right balance between under- and overreacting to evidence.** "Belief updating is to good forecasting as brushing and flossing are to good dental hygiene." Usually many small updates; occasionally a decisive big one. Tease subtle signals from noisy news flows while resisting wishful thinking.
5. **Look for the clashing causal forces at work in each problem.** Do a "dragonfly-eye" synthesis of arguments for and against; don't suppress dissonant possibilities.
6. **Strive to distinguish as many degrees of doubt as the problem permits but no more.** (Granularity — translate vague hunches into precise numeric probabilities; "many grades of maybe.")
7. **Strike the right balance between under- and overconfidence, between prudence and decisiveness.** Calibration and resolution are both required; hedging vacuously and reckless confidence are both failures.
8. **Look for the errors behind your mistakes but beware of rearview-mirror hindsight biases.** Conduct unflinching post-mortems — including on questions you got right for the wrong reasons.
9. **Bring out the best in others and let others bring out the best in you.** Master perspective-taking, precision questioning, and constructive confrontation (disagree without being disagreeable).
10. **Master the error-balancing bicycle.** These skills require deliberate practice with feedback, not just knowledge of the principles.
11. **(The meta-commandment) Don't treat commandments as commandments.** "It is impossible to lay down binding rules" (von Moltke); guidelines must yield to judgment about when they apply.

Sources: [Good Judgment — Tetlock's 10 Commandments](https://goodjudgment.com/philip-tetlocks-10-commandments-of-superforecasting/), [Farnam Street full text](https://fs.blog/ten-commandments-for-superforecasters/), [LessWrong copy](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/dvYeSKDRd68GcrWoe/ten-commandments-for-aspiring-superforecasters).

### 1.2 What distinguished superforecasters (exact numbers)
From Mellers et al. 2015, "Identifying and Cultivating Superforecasters as a Method of Improving Probabilistic Predictions," *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 10(3):267–281 ([PDF](https://stanford.edu/~knutson/nfc/mellers15.pdf)), read in full:

**Performance (avg of Years 2–3; supers vs. "top-team individuals" vs. all others):**
- Standardized Brier: **−0.34 vs −0.14 vs +0.04**
- Resolution: **0.40 vs 0.35 vs 0.32**
- Calibration error: **0.01 vs 0.03 vs 0.04** (supers' average gap between stated probability and observed frequency was one percentage point)
- AUC (discrimination): **96% vs 84% vs 75%**; supers' ROC at **300+ days** before resolution matched regular forecasters at only **60 days** out
- Supers were also the **fastest learners** within each season (steepest improvement slope), and stayed more accurate even for forecasts made within 4 minutes of first seeing a question.

**Granularity:** Supers used **57 unique probability values** (0–100 scale) vs **29–30** for the comparison groups; they were most likely to use multiples of **1%** (e.g., 17%, 28%, 83%), while others clustered on multiples of 10%. Rounding supers' forecasts **to the nearest 0.10 significantly worsened their Brier scores; rounding to 0.05 did not**. Rounding had no effect on regular forecasters until 0.33. (Same result later in Friedman et al. 2018, "The Value of Precision in Probability Assessment.") Implication: precision carries real information for elite forecasters — a bot should output granular probabilities, not round numbers.

**Frequent small updates:** Average forecasts per question — supers **2.77 (Y1), 5.64 (Y2), 6.70 (Y3)** vs all others **1.43 / 1.79 / 2.92**. Update frequency ("belief updating") was a top behavioral predictor of accuracy. Tetlock emphasizes updates are typically *small* increments.

**Dispositions/traits (correlations with accuracy; negative = better Brier):** Raven's matrices **−0.18**, extended Cognitive Reflection Test **−0.23**, numeracy **−0.16**, actively open-minded thinking (AOMT) **−0.12**, political knowledge **−0.12 to −0.18**. Supers scored higher on fluid intelligence (Raven's 9.13 vs 7.75), AOMT (6.01 vs 5.89 on 1–7), need for cognition, and were ≥1 SD above the general population on intelligence measures. A structural model of dispositional + situational + behavioral variables achieved a **multiple correlation of 0.64** with accuracy ([AI Impacts summary](https://aiimpacts.org/evidence-on-good-forecasting-practices-from-the-good-judgment-project/)).

**Scope sensitivity & debiasing:** Supers were far more scope-sensitive (overall scope effect **0.22 vs 0.12** for controls) — e.g., they gave 7% for "Turkey ratifies a new constitution by Feb 1, 2014" vs 35% by 2016, while controls gave a scope-insensitive 30%/49%. Supers were also **insensitive to irrelevant anchors** where controls were swayed. A bot should explicitly check: "would my probability change appropriately if the deadline or threshold changed?"

**Stability:** ~**70%** of superforecasters retained super status year over year; cross-year individual accuracy correlation ~**0.65** — skill, not luck ([AI Impacts](https://aiimpacts.org/evidence-on-good-forecasting-practices-from-the-good-judgment-project/)).

**Fox vs hedgehog:** From Tetlock's earlier *Expert Political Judgment* (2005; ~284 experts, ~28,000 predictions): experts overall barely beat simple extrapolation; "foxes" (many small models, eclectic, self-critical) reliably beat "hedgehogs" (one big organizing idea), and hedgehogs with deep expertise often did *worse* on long-range predictions in their own domain. Famous side finding (via [Jason Collins' course notes](https://corporate.jasoncollins.blog/better-forecasting)): when experts decomposed the Quebec-referendum scenario tree, their branch probabilities summed to **1.58** on average (foxes 2.09) — humans are incoherent under decomposition; a bot can enforce probability-sum coherence mechanically.

### 1.3 GJP quantitative findings: training, teaming, tracking, aggregation
GJP won both years of the IARPA ACE tournament, was **35–72% more accurate than other research teams**, beat its own control group by **60% (Year 1)** and **78% (Year 2)** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Good_Judgment_Project), [AI Impacts blog version](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/W94KjunX3hXAtZvXJ/evidence-on-good-forecasting-practices-from-the-good-1)).

**Training (the famous one-hour module).** Chang, Chen, Mellers & Tetlock 2016, "Developing expert political judgment," *Judgment and Decision Making* 11(5):509–526 ([PDF](https://jbaron.org/journal/16/16511/jdm16511.pdf)) — read in full:
- Training lasted **under one hour**, yet improved Brier scores by **6–11% consistently** over control: **Year 1: 10% (probability training) and 11% (scenario training); Year 2: 12%; Year 3: 6%; Year 4: 7%**. Raw mean Briers, Year 1: control **0.42** vs trained **0.38**. Effects persisted across the whole 9-month season.
- Year-1 probabilistic-reasoning training = **CHAMP**: **C**omparison classes, **H**istorical trends, **A**veraging (crowd wisdom), **M**athematical models, **P**redictable biases. Scenario training = **QUEST**: **Q**uestion views, **U**se plausible scenarios, **E**xplore assumptions, **S**everal assumptions should be considered, **T**ake heed of biases.
- Years 3–4 combined module = **CHAMPS KNOW** (Table 3, verbatim): **C**omparison classes; **H**unt for the right information; **A**djust and update forecasts when appropriate; **M**athematical and statistical models; **P**ost-mortem analysis; **S**elect the right questions to answer (Year 4: "Select the right level of effort to devote to each question"); **K**now the power players and their preferences; **N**orms and protocols of institutions; **O**ther perspectives should inform forecasts; **W**ildcards, accidents, and black swans.
- Which principles mattered: forecasts whose explanations invoked **comparison classes had mean Brier 0.17** — by far the best of the ten (next: Mathematical models and Select-effort at 0.26; "Hunt for information" 0.38; control condition 0.49). In ANOVA, **C was significantly associated with better accuracy; P (post-mortems) and O (other perspectives) with worse** (P likely because post-mortems follow bad forecasts). Probabilistic-reasoning principles (CHAMS) helped; political-knowledge principles (KNW) did not. **Base-rate/outside-view training is the single most valuable trainable skill.**

**Teaming.** Mellers et al. 2014, "Psychological Strategies for Winning a Geopolitical Forecasting Tournament," *Psychological Science* 25(5) ([journal](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797614524255)): teams were on average **23% more accurate than individuals**; training and teaming effects were **superadditive**. Rough chain reported by Tetlock: teams of ordinary forecasters beat the simple wisdom of the crowd by ~10%; prediction markets beat ordinary teams by ~20%; superforecaster teams beat prediction markets by **15–30%** ([AI Impacts blog post](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/W94KjunX3hXAtZvXJ/evidence-on-good-forecasting-practices-from-the-good-1)). (Caveat: Hauenstein et al. 2024 raise method-variance concerns about team-vs-individual comparisons — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39630638/).)

**Tracking.** Putting the top ~2% into elite teams of 12 "boosted accuracy far more than either of the two experimental manipulations — cognitive debiasing and collaborative teaming — administered alone or together" (Mellers et al. 2015). No regression to the mean in Years 2–3.

**The winning aggregation algorithm.** GJP's submitted aggregate ("All Surveys Logit") "takes the most recent forecasts from a selection of individuals…, weights them based on a forecaster's historical accuracy, expertise, and psychometric profile, and then extremizes the aggregate forecast (towards 1 or 0) using an optimized extremization coefficient" (quoted in [Arb Research's report](https://arbresearch.com/files/comparing_forecasters.pdf), fn. 4; method from Satopää et al. 2014). With GJP Year-1 data they used a **decay weight of 0.6 per day** on stale forecasts (Baron et al. 2014, fn. 6).

**The "30% better than intelligence analysts" claim — important caveat.** Arb Research's "Comparing Top Forecasters to Domain Experts" (March 2022, Sempere & Yagudin et al., [PDF](https://arbresearch.com/files/comparing_forecasters.pdf)) re-examined Goldstein et al. (2015): mean-of-means daily Brier — ICPM (classified intelligence-community prediction market) **0.23**, GJP simple average **0.32** (worse!), GJP prediction market **0.21** (statistically indistinguishable from ICPM), GJP best post-hoc aggregation **0.15**. So the famous edge came largely from the **aggregation algorithm**, not raw forecaster superiority; the unweighted forecaster average was *worse* than the IC. Arb is "fairly pessimistic (but uncertain)" on generalist forecasters strictly beating engaged domain experts; the best head-to-head study found parity, and "expertise plus basic forecasting training and active willingness to forecast regularly were enough to be on par with the best forecasters."

## 2. Aggregation Science

### 2.1 Why simple averaging is underconfident — the two reasons to extremize
Baron, Mellers, Tetlock, Stone & Ungar 2014, "Two Reasons to Make Aggregated Probability Forecasts More Extreme," *Decision Analysis* 11(2):133–145 ([PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/2015---two-reasons-to-make-aggregated-probability-forecasts_1.pdf)) — read in full:

- The transformation: **t(p) = p^a / (p^a + (1−p)^a)** — equivalent to multiplying log-odds by *a* (Karmarkar 1978). GJP commonly used **a = 2.5**.
- **Reason 1 — end-of-scale compression (affects the mean, not the median):** near 0/1, error is asymmetric (a true 0.99 can err downward by 0.99 but upward only by 0.01), so the *mean* of noisy forecasts is pulled toward 0.5. Extremizing the mean undoes this. The median is immune to this factor.
- **Reason 2 — regression toward 0.5 from individually-missing information (affects mean AND median AND mean-of-log-odds):** each rational forecaster shades toward 50% because she knows she lacks information; but the *group* collectively holds more information than any individual, so the aggregate should be more confident than the average individual. "Individual confidence … fails to take into account the wisdom of crowds." This is not a bias — individuals may be perfectly calibrated — it is a property of aggregation.
- Empirics (GJP Year 1, 1,973 forecasters, 86 binary questions): optimal *a* for self-rated **experts: mean 2.43, median 1.78**; **non-experts: mean 3.08, median 2.34**. Brier improvements from extremizing: experts' mean **0.187 → 0.148**; non-experts' mean **0.196 → 0.139**. Two rules of thumb fall out: **more expert/informed crowds need less extremizing**, and **the median needs less extremizing than the mean** (it's already more extreme).
- Bot-relevant aside: the paper notes irreducible uncertainty "generally decreas[es] as a question comes closer to being resolved" — so optimal extremization grows as resolution approaches.

### 2.2 Log-odds averaging (geometric mean of odds) vs probability averaging vs median
- **Geometric mean of odds (GMO) ≡ arithmetic mean of log-odds.** Jaime Sevilla, "When pooling forecasts, use the geometric mean of odds" (EA Forum, Sept 2021, [link](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/sMjcjnnpoAQCcedL2/when-pooling-forecasts-use-the-geometric-mean-of-odds)):
  - Empirics (Simon_M's analysis of ~850 resolved Metaculus binary questions): **GMO (recency-weighted): Brier 0.116, log score 0.370; arithmetic mean of probabilities: 0.122 / 0.392; median: 0.134** (worst of the three, though most outlier-robust); proprietary Metaculus prediction: 0.110.
  - Theory: GMO uniquely satisfies **external Bayesianity** (updating then pooling = pooling then updating), so the pooled crowd behaves like one coherent Bayesian.
  - GMO respects extreme, information-rich forecasts: averaging 10% and 0.1% in probability space gives ~5% (the 0.1% is effectively ignored); GMO treats 1-in-1,000 vs 1-in-10,000 as genuinely different. Differences between methods are negligible when all forecasts are in 10–90%.
- **Median:** robust to trolls/outliers and to the compression effect; the choice of Metaculus's Community Prediction — a **recency-weighted median** (weights increase with recency; ~half of forecasters must update to move it substantially) ([Metaculus FAQ](https://www.metaculus.com/faq/)).
- **Trimmed means:** Jose, Grushka-Cockayne & Lichtendahl 2014, "Trimmed Opinion Pools and the Crowd's Calibration Problem," *Management Science* 60(2):463–475 ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2175093)): the linear opinion pool grows *underconfident* as crowd diversity increases; **exterior trimming** (drop the most extreme forecasts on each side, then average) reduces variance and improves calibration, empirically beating the plain linear pool. This is exactly Samotsvety's practice (drop one most-extreme forecast per side, then GMO).

### 2.3 Extremizing: parameters, when it helps, when it hurts
- Satopää et al. 2014, "Combining multiple probability predictions using a simple logit model," *International Journal of Forecasting* 30(2):344–356: mean-of-logits with a multiplier; geopolitical data optimally aggregated with extremizing factor **d ∈ [1.161, 3.921]**; recommended in practice **a ≈ 2.5** (some follow-ups suggest ~2) ([summary](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/biL94PKfeHmgHY6qe/principled-extremizing-of-aggregated-forecasts), [paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260030454_Combining_multiple_probability_predictions_using_a_simple_logit_model)).
- **Principled n-dependent extremization** (Sevilla 2021, building on Neyman & Roughgarden 2021, [EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/biL94PKfeHmgHY6qe/principled-extremizing-of-aggregated-forecasts)): extremize the mean log-odds by **d(n) = n(√(3n²−3n+1) − 2)/(n²−n−1)**, i.e. **d ≈ 1.62 at n=10, 1.71 at n=50, → √3 ≈ 1.73** as n→∞. On 899 resolved Metaculus binary questions, Neyman extremization anchored to a historical baseline beat all other tested aggregators.
- **When extremizing hurts:**
  - Lichtendahl, Grushka-Cockayne, Jose & Winkler, "Bayesian Ensembles of Binary-Event Forecasts: When Is It Appropriate to Extremize or Anti-Extremize?" ([arXiv:1705.02391](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.02391)): with the right model of information overlap, sometimes you should **anti-extremize** (shrink toward the base rate). Roughly: extremize when forecasters hold *diverse, partially independent* information; anti-extremize/shrink when they share the same information or are individually overconfident.
  - Overfitting warning (Simon_M, quoted in Sevilla's post): "you will ALWAYS be able to find an extremizing parameter which will improve the forecasts [in hindsight] … unless they are perfectly calibrated. This will give you better predictions in hindsight but not better predictions going forward." On recent Metaculus data, extremized variants *underperformed* plain GMO — Metaculus's crowd is already well-calibrated/confident. **Extremize only with independent evidence the crowd is underconfident.**
  - Less extremizing needed for: expert crowds (Baron a=2.43 vs 3.08), medians (1.78 vs 2.43), small n, highly correlated forecasters.
- **Weighting:** Sevilla's practical guide ("My current best guess on how to aggregate forecasts," [EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/acREnv2Z5h4Fr5NWz/my-current-best-guess-on-how-to-aggregate-forecasts)): "the weighting you use for the predictions matters much more than the aggregation method" — weight by **recency** and **track record**. His decision rules: default = **geometric mean of odds**; use **arithmetic mean of probabilities** only when aggregating *mutually exclusive models* (P(E) = Σ P(model_i)·P(E|model_i)); use **median** when you can't exclude poorly-calibrated outliers; if you can identify outliers, "exclude them and take the geometric mean."

## 3. Reference Classes, Base Rates, and Debiasing

- **Outside view vs inside view** (Kahneman & Lovallo 1993; Kahneman & Tversky's "reference class forecasting," operationalized for megaprojects by Flyvbjerg): start from the statistical distribution of outcomes in a *comparison class of similar cases*, then adjust for case specifics. The inside view (building a causal story from the particulars) systematically underweights base rates and produces overconfidence/planning fallacy.
- **Superforecasters anchor on base rates, then adjust.** Tetlock's commandment 3; GJP training data shows comparison-class usage was *the* accuracy-correlated principle (Brier 0.17 vs 0.26–1.04 for other principles; [Chang et al. 2016](https://jbaron.org/journal/16/16511/jdm16511.pdf)). Chang et al.'s Iraq-WMD footnote is a model worked example: overall base rate of a country having nuclear/bio/chem programs ≈ **13% (26/194)**; Middle East reference class ≈ 17% (3/18) — "the large difference between the base-rate and the assessed probability should have at least given analysts pause."
- **Fermi decomposition:** break an intractable quantity into estimable multiplicative parts (commandment 2; Samotsvety's nuclear-risk decomposition below is the canonical practitioner example). Caveat from the Quebec finding: enforce coherence when recombining (probabilities of exclusive branches must sum to 1).
- **Premortem** (Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem," *HBR* 2007, [link](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)): assume your forecast/plan has already failed, generate the reasons why. Based on Mitchell, Russo & Pennington (1989): "prospective hindsight" increases ability to correctly identify reasons for future outcomes by **~30%**.
- **Consider the opposite** (Lord, Lepper & Preston 1984; Koriat, Lichtenstein & Fischhoff 1980): explicitly listing reasons your judgment could be *wrong* reduces overconfidence and confirmation bias; Chang et al. classify it among the most effective "process-based" debiasing techniques.
- **Ask yourself twice / dialectical bootstrapping** (Herzog & Hertwig 2009, cited in Chang et al. 2016): generate a second, independent-as-possible estimate (assume the first was wrong; consider different assumptions) and average — a one-agent wisdom of crowds. Directly implementable as multiple independent LLM samples.

## 4. Scoring Rule Theory

- **Proper scoring rules:** a scoring rule is (strictly) proper iff expected score is (uniquely) maximized by reporting your true belief — no hedging incentive. Both Brier and log are strictly proper ([Wikipedia: Scoring rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scoring_rule), [Statistical Odds & Ends](https://statisticaloddsandends.wordpress.com/2021/03/27/what-is-a-proper-scoring-rule/)).
- **Brier score:** mean squared error, B = (p − o)², range 0–1 for binary (GJP used the original two-outcome sum form, range 0–2; e.g., p=0.75 on the realized outcome → (1−0.75)² + (0−0.25)² = 0.125). Always-50% scores 0.25 (one-outcome form). Decomposes into **calibration + resolution + irreducible variability** (Murphy & Winkler 1987) — useful diagnostics for a bot's track record.
- **Log score:** S = ln p(outcome). Unbounded: p→0 on a realized outcome gives −∞. The Brier score is approximately a **second-order Taylor approximation of the log score** for well-calibrated forecasts near 0/1 ([scoringutils vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/scoringutils/vignettes/scoring-rules.html)); they differ mid-range: Brier penalizes over- and underconfidence symmetrically in probability space; log score penalizes overconfidence much more harshly than underconfidence near the extremes.
- **Hedging math (why "shade toward 50%" is wrong under both, but extreme claims are riskier under log):** because both rules are proper, *any* deviation from your true belief loses expected score. But the loss profile differs. If your true belief is 0.99 and you report 0.95, your expected Brier loss is (0.99−0.95)² = 0.0016 — trivial. Under log score the same hedge costs little in expectation too, but the *downside variance* differs: if you report 0.99 and the event fails, log score = ln(0.01) ≈ −4.6 (catastrophic, vs Brier's bounded ~0.98). Practical asymmetry: under log scoring, the expected-value-maximizing report is still your true belief, but **model misspecification/overconfidence near 0 and 1 is fatal under log and survivable under Brier**. Hence the practitioner rules: under log-type scoring (Metaculus), never output 0% or 1%; clamp to something like [1%, 99%] (or wider only with overwhelming evidence); under Brier-type scoring, fine-grained extremes matter less.
- **Platform specifics:** Metaculus's baseline/peer scores are log-based; its legacy "relative score" likewise. Metaculus also applies **score truncation** for questions that can resolve early: days between early resolution and scheduled close count with score 0, deliberately so forecasters can't game "will X happen by date" questions by sitting at high probabilities early and dropping later ([Metaculus Scores FAQ](https://www.metaculus.com/help/scores-faq/), [Medium primer](https://metaculus.medium.com/a-primer-on-the-metaculus-scoring-rule-eb9a974cd204)). Time-averaging of daily scores means **updating promptly matters as much as final accuracy** — a stale forecast bleeds score every day.
- Baron et al. 2014 Appendix A gives a decision-theoretic justification of Brier: if action thresholds are uniformly distributed in (0,1), minimizing Brier maximizes expected utility of decisions taken on the forecast.

## 5. Practitioner Tips from Elite Forecasters

### 5.1 Good Judgment / Metaculus official guidance
- Good Judgment's keys: **talent-spotting, training, teaming, aggregation** ([Science of Superforecasting](https://goodjudgment.com/about/the-science-of-superforecasting/)); their commercial training is the CHAMPS KNOW lineage.
- Metaculus FAQ ([link](https://www.metaculus.com/faq/)): Community Prediction = recency-weighted median of latest forecast per user; "the Community Prediction is often better than any individual predictor"; own your failures — review both calibration and resolution against the community's track record. Metaculus maintains a community "Forecasting Tips and Strategies" notebook (RyanBeck, June 2024, [link](https://www.metaculus.com/notebooks/25399/forecasting-tips-and-strategies/)) and a [Prediction Resources page](https://www.metaculus.com/help/prediction-resources/). Standard Metaculus community heuristics (recurring across these resources and forum discussions): start from the base rate; favor the **status quo / "no" on event questions** (most proposed events don't happen); read resolution criteria *literally* and forecast the criteria, not the vibe of the title; check the current community prediction as a sanity anchor and only deviate with reasons; update often.

### 5.2 Samotsvety's process (verified)
From "Samotsvety Nuclear Risk Forecasts — March 2022" ([nunosempere.com](https://nunosempere.com/blog/2022/03/10/samotsvety-nuclear-risk-forecasts-march-2022/), [EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/KRFXjCqqfGQAYirm5/samotsvety-nuclear-risk-forecasts-march-2022)) and "Samotsvety's AI risk forecasts" ([EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/EG9xDM8YRz4JN4wMN/samotsvety-s-ai-risk-forecasts)):
1. **Independent forecasts first** (8 forecasters, surveys, no anchoring on each other), often each with their own decomposition.
2. **Decomposition into multiplicative factors** — e.g., P(death in London from nuclear explosion in a month) = P(NATO/Russia nuclear exchange in month) × P(escalation reaches central London) × P(informed actors can't evacuate in time) × fatality rate. "Different forecasters preferred different decompositions" — diversity of decompositions is treated as a feature.
3. **Discussion phase** to surface considerations (but final numbers remain individual).
4. **Aggregation: drop the single most extreme forecast on each end, then take the geometric mean of odds** (their stated standard). They alternate between "the probability of the best model when one exists" and GMO "when it's a risk that requires more subjective judgment."
5. **Sanity checks:** commissioned external red-teaming (Clay Graubard on question framing, Daniel Filan independent estimate); computed separate aggregates for subgroups (e.g., non-EA members) to test in-group bias; reported ranges including/excluding extremes (e.g., 24 micromorts after removing extremes; range 0.0001–112.5 with them).
- Founders Nuño Sempere & Misha Yagudin; members were repeat winners of CSET-Foretell/INFER ("Samotsvety's track record over ~1,000 questions... relative Brier ~4x better than the next team" is their reported INFER result).

### 5.3 Individual practitioner lessons
- **Linch Zhang**, "Some learnings I had from forecasting in 2020" ([EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/kAMfrLJwHpCdDSqsj/some-learnings-i-had-from-forecasting-in-2020)): forming *good* outside views is genuinely hard (the skill is finding the *right* reference class, not just any); people over-trust official data/ontologies in out-of-distribution situations; the EA community overrates superforecasting-the-brand; trained intuitions matter ("most forecasting mistakes are due to emotional rather than technical reasons").
- **Eli Lifland** (Samotsvety, #1 all-time on RAND Forecasting Initiative), "Personal forecasting retrospective 2020–2022" ([foxy-scout.com](https://www.foxy-scout.com/retro/)): repeatedly *underestimated recurrence* (new COVID waves) — beware "this phase is the last one" reasoning; study history of the phenomenon class; his continuous-question tails were "systematically not fat enough" — widen distributions; be skeptical of simplistic equilibrium models ("herd immunity and it peters out").
- **Nuño Sempere's Forecasting Newsletter** ([forecasting.substack.com](https://forecasting.substack.com/)) is the running archive of this community's technique discussions.
- **Aggregation-of-experts tip** repeated across Sevilla/Samotsvety/GJP: elicit *odds or log-odds* mentally for extreme events (1-in-100 vs 1-in-1,000 distinctions matter); probability-space intuition fails below ~5%.

### 5.4 Directly LLM-relevant precedent
- Halawi, Zhang, Yueh-Han & Steinhardt, "Approaching Human-Level Forecasting with Language Models" (NeurIPS 2024, [arXiv:2402.18563](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563)): retrieval (search → relevance-filter → summarize) + **scratchpad reasoning prompts** (restate question + resolution criteria + dates; list arguments for and against; consider base rates; produce calibrated probability) + **ensembling multiple model forecasts via trimmed mean**, with fine-tuning for calibration. System Brier ≈ 0.179 vs human crowd ≈ 0.149 on post-cutoff questions, beating the crowd in selective settings (e.g., early in question life, when retrieval is rich). Their design choices are essentially the GJP playbook encoded in prompts — independent samples ≈ a crowd; trimmed aggregation ≈ Jose et al.; scratchpad ≈ CHAMPS KNOW.

## 6. Time Decay, Hazard Rates, and "Burning Down" Probability

- **The core math.** For "Will X happen by date Y?": if your event has roughly constant hazard rate, and your probability for the original window of T units is P, then the per-unit hazard r solves (1−r)^T = 1−P, and with t units remaining the correct current forecast is **P_now = 1 − (1−r)^t**. Absent news, the probability **must drift toward 0** as the deadline approaches (and symmetrically toward 1 for "will X persist/keep not-happening" questions). A forecast that sits still while the clock runs is implicitly *raising* its per-day hazard estimate every day.
- **Formal practitioner treatment:** Nuño Sempere, "Adjusting probabilities for the passage of time, using Squiggle models" (LessWrong, [link](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/j8o6sgRerE3tqNWdj/adjusting-probabilities-for-the-passage-of-time-using)) gives three escalating methods: (1) **naive exponential decay** (constant hazard, formula above); (2) **distribution over decay rates** (specify optimistic/pessimistic bounds on the rate, propagate to a distribution — hedges model uncertainty about the hazard); (3) **Laplace's rule of succession** — with k events observed in n elapsed periods, per-period probability ≈ **(k+1)/(n+2)**, which automatically decays as event-free time accumulates and is more principled when the hazard itself is being learned. He proposes platforms auto-apply decay schedules between manual updates — directly implementable in a bot.
- **Non-constant hazards:** real questions often have *lumpy* hazard concentrated at known dates (elections, court rulings, scheduled launches, OPEC meetings). The right model is a hazard schedule, not exponential decay: probability stays flat between catalysts and steps down sharply when a catalyst passes without the event. Tetlock's superforecasters' "frequent small updates" on deadline questions are largely this burn-down done manually.
- **Scoring interaction:** because Metaculus time-averages daily log scores (with truncation for early resolution — [Scores FAQ](https://www.metaculus.com/help/scores-faq/)), failing to burn down probability is continuously penalized; a bot should re-emit decayed forecasts on a schedule even with no news. Common observed community failure: forecasts on "by date Y" questions stay too high too long, then crash in the final weeks.
- **Related:** Baron et al. 2014 note irreducible uncertainty shrinks as resolution nears — aggregates can justifiably be extremized more aggressively late in a question's life. And scope-sensitivity checks (Mellers 2015, Table 4) are the static version of the same skill: P(X by 2014) must be coherently less than P(X by 2016).

## 7. Synthesis: Prompt-Encodable Checklist for an LLM Forecasting Bot

Everything above compresses into bot-design rules, each traceable to evidence:

1. **Triage prompt:** first classify the question — near-certain, pure noise, or Goldilocks; spend compute accordingly (Commandment 1; "S" was one of three accuracy-linked CHAMPS KNOW principles).
2. **Read the resolution criteria literally**; restate question, dates, thresholds before reasoning (Metaculus guidance; Halawi scratchpad).
3. **Outside view first:** force the model to name 2–3 candidate reference classes, give a base rate (with counts where possible), *then* adjust with inside-view evidence. Largest known trainable effect (comparison-class forecasts: Brier 0.17 vs 0.49 control).
4. **Fermi-decompose** hard questions into factors; multiply; but enforce coherence (branch probabilities sum to 1; P(by 2026) ≥ P(by 2025)) — humans fail this (sum = 1.58); a bot can check mechanically.
5. **Clashing causal forces / consider-the-opposite:** require explicit "strongest argument for," "strongest argument against," and a premortem ("it's resolution day and my forecast was badly wrong — why?"). (+30% reason-identification from prospective hindsight.)
6. **Status-quo and hazard reasoning for deadline questions:** compute the remaining-window probability from a per-period hazard, decay it daily absent news, with hazard mass concentrated on scheduled catalysts; use Laplace (k+1)/(n+2) when the rate is unknown.
7. **Scope-sensitivity self-test:** ask the model what it would forecast if the deadline/threshold were doubled or halved; flag insensitivity (supers 0.22 vs controls 0.12).
8. **Granular output:** report to 1% granularity (rounding supers to 10% measurably hurt them); never 0%/1% under log scoring — clamp to ~[1, 99]% or tighter per domain; under Brier scoring extremes are cheaper.
9. **Self-crowd:** sample multiple independent forecasts (different decompositions/personas/retrieval seeds = information diversity), then aggregate by **trimmed geometric mean of odds** (drop the extreme(s) on each end, mean the log-odds) — Samotsvety + Jose et al. + Sevilla. Weight more recent/better-track-record runs if applicable.
10. **Extremize cautiously:** only when ensemble members are diverse and individually shading toward 50% (Baron's regression factor); d ≈ 1.5–2.5 historically (Satopää [1.16–3.92], Neyman d→√3 ≈ 1.73); skip extremizing for already-confident, calibrated, or highly correlated ensembles — fitted extremization overfits (Simon_M).
11. **Small frequent updates:** prefer many incremental updates to rare jumps (supers: 6.7 forecasts/question vs 2.9; daily-averaged scoring rewards freshness); but allow decisive large updates on diagnostic news (Commandment 4's balance).
12. **Anchor on the community/market price when available** and demand explicit reasons to deviate; remember the unweighted GJP crowd *lost* to the intelligence community — the edge is in aggregation and updating, not raw opinion (Goldstein et al. via Arb).
13. **Track record loop:** log every forecast, compute calibration and resolution (Brier decomposition), run post-mortems on misses *and* lucky hits (Commandment 8) — but note post-mortem reasoning correlates with bad scores only because it follows them; the loop's value is in changed future behavior.
14. **Watch the known LLM-relevant failure modes from human data:** underconfidence of pooled averages, fat-enough tails on continuous quantities (Lifland), recurrence underestimation, overtrust of official ontologies out-of-distribution (Zhang), and wishful/emotional contamination — "most forecasting mistakes are due to emotional rather than technical reasons."
